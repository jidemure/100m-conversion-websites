# $100M Conversion Websites

Use this skill to make a page earn trust and action—not merely look expensive. Treat “$100M-caliber” as a conversion and value-perception standard, not a visual style. Design, copy, proof, and interaction should all reduce uncertainty and make the next step feel relevant, credible, and worthwhile.

## Operating rules

- Start with the business outcome, offer, traffic source, audience, buying stage, and primary conversion event.
- Never invent proof, testimonials, customer counts, certifications, guarantees, performance data, or scarcity. Mark missing evidence and request it.
- Use persuasion to clarify real value, not to deceive, pressure vulnerable people, or conceal material conditions.
- Prefer specific customer language and observable outcomes over generic marketing adjectives.
- Make every visual or interaction earn its place by improving comprehension, trust, desire, or action.
- Separate hypotheses from validated findings. Recommend A/B tests when evidence is uncertain.
- For health, finance, legal, safety, or other regulated claims, use conservative wording and require substantiation/review.

## Workflow

### 1. Diagnose before writing

Create a short conversion brief:

- **Offer:** what is sold, to whom, at what price or commitment, with what mechanism and constraints.
- **ICP and moment:** exact audience, situation, awareness level, trigger, objections, alternatives, and desired outcome.
- **Traffic promise:** what the visitor was told before arriving and what they expect to find.
- **Primary action:** one dominant CTA; define secondary actions only when they support the main path.
- **Proof inventory:** quantified results, case studies, reviews, demonstrations, credentials, process evidence, guarantees, and risk reducers.
- **Friction inventory:** unclear language, price anxiety, perceived risk, effort, technical confusion, poor fit, and unanswered objections.

If the brief is incomplete, make the smallest reasonable assumptions, label them, and list the questions that could change the page.

### 2. Build the message around the six principles

Apply all six, but weight them according to the audience and offer.

1. **Framing effect.** Present the same true information from the customer’s most useful perspective. Reframe cost as the value or investment only when the value is concrete. Reframe features as outcomes, passive proof as participation, and generic categories as meaningful life results. Preserve accuracy; do not use euphemisms to hide price or risk.
2. **Life Force Eight.** Choose one primary desire and, at most, two supporting desires. Map the offer to: survival/enjoyment of life; comfortable living; sexual companionship/attractiveness; freedom from fear, pain, and danger; status/winning; care/protection of loved ones; enjoyment of food/drink; social approval. Express the selected desire through copy, imagery, proof, and the offer—not through stereotypes or unsupported fear.
3. **Labor illusion.** Make valuable work visible. Explain the decisions, materials, quality controls, steps, expertise, human review, time saved, and safeguards that differentiate the offer. Tie each detail to why the customer benefits. Do not merely add process theater or needless length.
4. **Similarity attraction.** Mirror the audience’s real context. Use authentic people, situations, language, objections, and desired outcomes that the audience recognizes. Prefer customer research, interviews, reviews, communities, support logs, and usability sessions to invented “brand voice.”
5. **Cognitive fluency.** Make the page easy to understand and scan. Use one connected idea per headline, concrete words, short paragraphs, clear hierarchy, informative subheads, bullets, progressive disclosure, and meaningful labels. Aim for plain language, often around a 6th–8th grade reading level, unless the audience requires technical precision.
6. **Offer value.** Trust is necessary but insufficient. Increase perceived value by strengthening the desired outcome, confidence of success, speed, convenience, support, risk reversal, and fit while reducing effort, delay, uncertainty, and hidden conditions. State what is included, excluded, guaranteed, refundable, or required.

### 3. Architect the page

Choose the shortest structure that answers the visitor’s real questions. A strong default is:

1. **Above the fold:** audience + desired outcome + differentiated mechanism + primary CTA + immediate proof or risk reducer.
2. **Problem recognition:** show the visitor’s situation in their language; avoid exaggerated pain.
3. **Outcome and mechanism:** explain what changes and why this approach works.
4. **Value proposition sections:** expose the meaningful work, differentiators, and customer benefits.
5. **Relevant proof:** place customer-specific testimonials, results, demonstrations, or credentials beside the claim they support.
6. **How it works:** make the process concrete, bounded, and easy to imagine.
7. **Objections and risk:** answer price, fit, timing, safety, implementation, switching, and “what if it fails?”
8. **Offer and CTA:** summarize the exchange, bonuses or support when real, terms, guarantee, and next step.
9. **FAQ and final CTA:** resolve remaining friction without introducing new strategic claims.

For a product page, service page, lead-generation page, or long-form sales page, adapt the sequence rather than copying it mechanically.

### 4. Write the first draft

For every major section, specify the visitor question being answered, the psychological principle being used, the claim and its evidence, the desired emotion or interpretation, the CTA or next step, and the testable hypothesis.

Use a message matrix before polishing language:

`Audience situation → desired outcome → barrier → proof → mechanism → wording → CTA`

Use concrete transformations such as:

- “See why customers love us” → “Join [verified number] people who now [specific outcome].”
- “Upgrade your kitchen” → “Upgrade the heart of your home.”
- “Low-cost, high-profit workshop” → show the real activity, time, margin, and assumptions behind the economics.

Treat these as patterns, not copy to reuse blindly. Never copy a source brand’s claims or imply that an example’s result will transfer automatically.

### 5. Design for comprehension and belief

- Use imagery that shows the customer’s recognizable context, not generic stock aspiration.
- Put proof adjacent to the claim it validates.
- Turn complex value into scannable visuals: process maps, comparison blocks, calculators, timelines, specifications, or before/after evidence when truthful and permitted.
- Use typography, spacing, contrast, and motion to guide attention; do not let them delay comprehension or obscure the CTA.
- Keep the first screen focused: one audience, one promise, one primary action.
- Make mobile, accessibility, loading speed, keyboard navigation, and readable contrast part of conversion quality.

### 6. Validate and iterate

Before delivery, run the checklist in `references/qa-checklist.md`. Then propose a test plan with a hypothesis, control and variant, primary metric and guardrail metrics, minimum evidence or decision rule, audience/traffic segment, implementation notes, and the result that would falsify the hypothesis.

Test high-leverage uncertainty first: promise, audience fit, mechanism, proof, offer, CTA, then visual details. Do not call a result conclusive without adequate traffic, clean measurement, and a defined decision rule.

## Required output formats

When creating a page, deliver a conversion brief with assumptions and open questions; a message architecture showing the section sequence and job of each section; a copy/design specification for each section, including principle, evidence, imagery, and CTA; a proof and claim ledger separating verified facts, customer language, hypotheses, and missing evidence; and a QA and experiment plan using the reference checklist.

When auditing a page, report the highest-impact issues first and rewrite only the sections that need changing. Explain the expected mechanism of improvement.
